1. Reading a Rating

Elo89 gives each rikishi a number: his rating. The simplest way to read it is that higher means better. More precisely, if two rikishi were to meet, Elo89 would favour the one with the higher rating.

How strongly it favours him depends on the gap between their ratings. A small gap means the model regards them as closely matched. A large gap means it expects the higher-rated rikishi to win more often.

Suppose two rikishi have ratings of 2300 and 2100. On Elo89’s scale, that 200-point difference gives the higher-rated wrestler an estimated winning chance of about 76%. He is a clear favourite, but his opponent still has roughly a one-in-four chance. An upset would not, by itself, show that the ratings were wrong.

The numbers 2300 and 2100 have no special meaning individually. Ratings of 1800 and 1600 give exactly the same estimated chance, because the difference is again 200 points. A rating is not an amount of strength, and a wrestler rated 2400 is not twice as strong as one rated 1200.

Where the numbers come from

Elo89 starts each rikishi with a rating informed by his banzuke rank, then updates it using his recorded bout results. It takes account of whom he faced as well as whether he won.

An expected win produces a small increase. Beating someone much more highly rated produces a larger increase. A loss works in the opposite direction: losing to the favourite costs less than losing to someone the model expected you to beat.

For example, suppose our two rikishi rated 2300 and 2100 are both maegashira. Elo89 favours the higher-rated wrestler, giving him about a 76% chance of winning.

  • If the favourite wins, he gains about 3.6 points, reaching 2303.6. His opponent loses the same amount, falling to 2096.4.
  • If the underdog wins, he gains about 11.4 points, reaching 2111.4. The favourite falls to 2288.6.

The unexpected result produces the larger adjustment. But even after the upset, the original favourite remains higher rated: one bout adds evidence; it does not erase everything that came before.

This gives us a running account of performance rather than a count of wins. Two rikishi with identical win–loss records can have different ratings because they faced different opponents and arrived with different histories.

Elo89 also makes common adjustments to the rating population between tournaments. Consequently, a change between two published ratings is not necessarily all earned or lost through bouts. We will explain those adjustments later.

What a rating can tell you

A rating is a tool for comparison. It gives you a way to ask how the evidence of previous results places one rikishi relative to another.

It does not tell you why a wrestler won, whether he is carrying an injury, or how he will perform tomorrow. A small rating advantage should not be mistaken for a decisive difference, and even a substantial favourite can lose.

Nor is a rating an official position. Sumo already has those in the banzuke. Elo89 offers a complementary way to examine performance—and understanding why that is useful is our next question.

Elo89’s calculation is deterministic: given the same starting ratings, model settings and sequence of results, it produces exactly the same ratings. The choices involved in designing the system are fixed in advance; applying it requires no further judgement about an individual rikishi.

That does not make its forecasts certain, or its assumptions beyond question. It means that we can trace how its numbers arise. This gives us a useful contrast with chii, where judgement remains part of deciding each new banzuke.
