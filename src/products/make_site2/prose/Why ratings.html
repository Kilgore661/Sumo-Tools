In sumo, official standing is measured by chii, the Japanese word for rank. A chii is written as a division, a number, and a side: for example, “Maegashira 2 West” (M2w). The top rank classes are, in descending order, Yokozuna (Y), Ozeki (O), Sekiwake (S), Komusubi (K), and Maegashira (M). East is generally considered slightly more prestigious than West, and lower numbers denote higher standing; thus, M2w is above M3e, but below M2e. There can be at most one rikishi at any given chii, so the top rikishi is (usually) the Y1e.

Chii are decided by the Japan Sumo Association (JSA). The exact method is not published, but the JSA is made up of former rikishi with deep knowledge of sumo’s history and traditions. Chii are based mainly on a rikishi’s current chii and his latest results, with the JSA using its experience to weigh context in close calls.

The current system of placing rikishi at a specific chii has been used in some form since at least 1757. It is traditional, meaningful, and attractively mysterious to many non-Japanese fans, myself included. I do not want to see it replaced.

But chii are not an objective rating system in the same way as, say, a football league table is. To a newcomer, the logic behind some ranking decisions can seem ineffable. That is part of the appeal, but it also leaves room for a different kind of tool.

That is why I adapted Elo ratings to sumo. Ratings ask a different question: not “where has the JSA placed this rikishi?”, but “how strong does the record suggest he is?” When rating and chii diverge, the difference can be interesting in both directions. It may expose the limitations of the rating model, or it may highlight something important about what the JSA appears to value in ranking decisions.

These ratings are not intended to replace the banzuke. They are a second lens: a tool for curiosity. By placing the two systems side by side, we can get a richer view of the sport than either one provides alone.
