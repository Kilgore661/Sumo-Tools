In sumo, the top rank classes are, in descending order, Yokozuna (Y), Ozeki (O), Sekiwake (S), Komusubi (K), and Maegashira (M).

The official standing is measured by chii, the Japanese word for rank. A chii comprises a rank class, a number, and a side: for example, “Maegashira 2 West” (M2w). East is generally considered slightly more prestigious than West, and lower numbers denote higher standing. Thus, M2w is above M3e, but below M2e. There can be at most one rikishi at any given chii, so the top rikishi is (usually) the Y1e.

Chii are decided by the Japan Sumo Association (JSA). The JSA is made up of former rikishi with deep knowledge of sumo’s history and traditions. It is clear that chii are based mainly on a rikishi’s current chii and his latest results, with the JSA using its experience to weigh context in close calls, but the exact method of determining chii is not published.

The current system of placing rikishi at a specific chii has been used in some form since at least 1757. It is traditional, meaningful, and attractively mysterious to many non-Japanese fans, myself included. But chii are not an objective rating system in the same way as, say, the points tally in a soccer league table, or the percentage of wins in baseball. To a newcomer, the logic behind some ranking decisions in sumo can seem ineffable. That is part of the appeal, but it also leaves room for a different kind of tool.

I used to think of the banzuke as a flawed attempt to rank rikishi by current performance. I now think that is the wrong way to see it. There is a real sense in which the banzuke is not trying to be a performance rating table at all. It is an official ordering of rikishi within the traditions, constraints, and judgements of sumo. It must place every rikishi somewhere, and it must do so inside a fixed historical form.

That does not make the banzuke wrong. It means that it is answering a different question. The banzuke asks where a rikishi should stand in the world of sumo, given his current rank, his latest record, the available places, and the judgement of the people responsible for making the ranking. A rating asks a narrower question: how strong does the record suggest he is?

This distinction is especially important for non-Japanese fans like me. It is easy to approach sumo as if it were only a competitive sport and then become frustrated when the banzuke does not behave like a league table. But sumo is not only a competitive sport. It is also a tradition, an institution, and a way of ordering a particular world. Ratings are useful not because they replace that world, but because they provide a second way to look at performance within it.

When rating and chii diverge, the difference can be interesting in both directions. It may expose the limitations of the rating model, or it may highlight something important about what the JSA appears to value in ranking decisions. So ratings are not intended to replace the banzuke. They are a second lens: a tool for curiosity. By placing the two systems side by side, we can get a richer view of the sport than either one provides alone.
