# Elo-89 normalisation: investigation and interpretation

## Conclusion and purpose of this account

We regard the observed effects of population normalisation as acceptable and
find no sufficient reason in this investigation to change Elo-89. That is an
informed judgement about the model's use, not a claim that normalisation has
no effect on every reported rating change.

The headline observation is that every eligible Makuuchi and Juryo observation
across 51 recent sliding 12-basho windows had a net normalisation contribution
within ±1% of its end rating. The full-history analysis supplies the wider
context, including larger contributions and exceptions in the lowest divisions.

This account is separate from the [overall Elo-89 appraisal](../../docs/story/22%20Elo-89%20Rating%20System%20Appraisal.md).
It preserves the question originally marked TBD, the interpretations considered,
the choices made during exploration, and the limits of the resulting conclusion.
Future work can revisit those choices without reopening the entire appraisal
or mistaking an exploratory convention for a necessary property of Elo.

## The original TBD

The appraisal observed that reported rating changes are not wholly derived
from bouts: common population adjustments also contribute. The initial
expectation was that their contribution would generally be small compared
with variation generated by competition over the same period, particularly
in Makuuchi. An experiment remained to be specified.

Two questions subsequently needed separating:

1. Can normalisation noticeably affect the interpretation of a particular
   reported rating change?
2. Is its contribution large enough to undermine our practical interpretation
   of the ratings themselves?

The answer to the first can be yes without requiring a negative judgement on
the second. This distinction is central to the conclusion. The exploration
expanded beyond the original comparison with bout-derived changes; its later
results should not be presented as though they were the only test originally
specified.

The author already considered Elo-89 useful and credible. This investigation
was not a publication gate. Even an appreciable but non-disastrous effect
could be accepted, explained openly, and left available for readers to challenge
with a better-supported alternative. The diagnostic code therefore lives in
its own package and consumes existing production outputs without changing the
rating model, fitting P1 again, or regenerating the website.

## Which normalisation?

Three operations must not be confused:

- The fixed-point construction helps determine the P1 entrant-rating map.
  Once adopted, that map is fixed; its construction adjustments are not
  recurring chii-based updates to individual wrestlers.
- Before a basho, departures and initialisations change the represented
  population. Elo-89 adds a common constant to restore its fixed mean.
- After the basho's eligible bouts, Elo-89 again restores that mean with a
  common constant, correcting the net effect of unequal update rates.

The last two operations are the subject of this investigation. Each preserves
all rating gaps, ordering and matchup probabilities among the active wrestlers
at the instant of application. Removing departures, adding entrants and
processing bouts are separate operations.

Absence also needs a precise meaning. A wrestler missing bouts while remaining
on the banzuke retains his rating, subject to common adjustments. Someone who
leaves the represented population and later returns is reinitialised from P1.
The author accepts this distinction. Returning episodes must be identified in
the accounting so a reset is not mislabelled as normalisation.

## Ratings, gaps and numerical labels

A list ordered by rating, with the leader labelled zero and each subsequent
entry recording the gap to its predecessor, contains the information required
to reconstruct ratings up to a common additive constant. It therefore also
contains the information used for Elo matchup probabilities. Individual rating
numbers are a convenient presentation of this relational information.

This makes the arbitrary origin relevant to the interpretation of percentages.
Adding a constant to everyone's rating changes a contribution/rating ratio
without changing competitive relationships. The ratios used below are therefore
the author's practical benchmarks on the published Elo-89 scale, not invariant
measures of predictive distortion. This qualification does not prevent using
them consistently as an explicit descriptive convention.

A specific example helped establish what practical insignificance means here.
Ratings of 2300 and 2304 imply an expected score of about 49.42% versus 50.58%
under Elo-89's q=400 formula. The wrestlers are effectively evenly matched.
If, in an accounting decomposition, four points of the latter rating came from
normalisation and none of the former did, removing that discrepancy would
make the accounting values equal without meaningfully changing the interpretation.

An ordinal table can make the same small difference look more consequential:
fourth versus fifth is categorical and conceals the small gap. That does not
make the underlying difference competitively large. Also, a hypothetical
unequal accumulated contribution is distinct from a single common shift,
which cannot move one active wrestler past another.

## What was measured

The saved production run covers January 1989 through July 2026, with 224
represented basho. For each eligible endpoint pair, the diagnostic reconstructs:

```text
end rating minus start rating
    = sum of observed bout updates
    + sum of pre-basho common adjustments received
    + sum of post-basho common adjustments received
    + any reinitialisation contribution
```

Start and end observations are processed basho-end ratings. A window of n
basho includes the following n represented basho, excluding contributions
already contained in the start rating. Windows containing reinitialisation
are accounted for separately and excluded from the continuous-window results
reported here. No adjustments are assigned during an absence from the
represented population.

The reconstruction reconciled 985,401 wrestler-window observations with a
maximum numerical residual of approximately 7.59e-12 points. All 3,822 latest
site rows matched within the site's rounding precision. These checks establish
the accounting, not the practical significance of its terms.

Subtracting normalisation holds the observed bout updates fixed. It does not
calculate what the ratings would have been under a different normalisation
policy. Such a policy can affect survivors' relation to future entrants and
therefore subsequent probabilities and bout updates. A counterfactual replay
was discussed but was not required for this investigation's conclusion.

Nor is a 12-basho contribution the total accumulated over a wrestler's career.
That would require a separate accounting since initialisation. Neither an
explicit pairwise gap report nor that lifetime decomposition is supplied by
the results below.

## Why we moved from absolute summaries to signed contributions

The first summaries emphasised average magnitudes and ratios to the magnitudes
of bout-derived changes. They answered how large the contribution in a typical
window was, irrespective of direction. The author regarded this as the wrong
emphasis for understanding net effects: +2 followed by -2 should contribute zero.

Cancellation within each window was already part of the calculation. However,
taking magnitudes before summarising across windows discarded further directional
information. The subsequent interpretation therefore uses signed contributions,
signed means and signed distributions. The earlier magnitude-based results
remain available but do not provide the conclusion of this account.

The later ±1% and ±5% counts ask whether a net signed contribution lies inside
a symmetric interval. They still allow positive and negative adjustments
within a window to cancel; they are not sums of unsigned adjustments.

## What the common shifts show

End-of-basho corrections were small: their signed range was -0.1534 to +0.1433
points and their mean was -0.0216. Pre-basho shifts were larger, ranging from
-8.6405 to +30.4014 points, with a signed mean of +1.1814. Most of the issue
therefore concerns population changes before the bouts.

Across the full 224-basho record, the pre-basho shifts sum to +264.640 and the
post-basho shifts to -4.829, leaving +259.810 points in the common-shift index.
For January 2016 through July 2026, the corresponding combined sum is only
+12.275 points over 63 basho, or +0.195 per basho. Cancellation is substantial,
particularly recently, but is not complete across the whole historical record.

These are indices of common shifts, not amounts to subtract from every current
wrestler. Individuals receive shifts only while represented. A positive sum
does not itself demonstrate rating inflation: the operations restore the
population mean, and this experiment has not computed an alternative replay.

The May influx provides useful context. Excluding the artificial initial
population in January 1989, first appearances in the represented population
averaged 61.9 per May over the full record, 36.6 since 2016 and 31.2 since 2021.
May remained the largest intake in each year from 2021 through 2026. Returns
were counted separately. These are dataset first appearances, not independently
verified career debut dates.

Historically, the mean combined May adjustment was +12.178 points, while
January, March, September and November had negative means. This supports the
interpretation of a seasonal component that partly cancels. Headcount alone
does not determine the correction: entrant ratings and departures matter too.

An individual historical example remains important. In May 1992, Konishiki's
bout contribution was -26.132 points and his combined normalisation contribution
was +30.363, giving a displayed change of +4.231. A claim that every positive
displayed change necessarily represents positive bout-derived performance
would be wrong. The example is compatible with accepting the rating model.

## Window choice and the signed Makuuchi results

The diagnostic implements the site's 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 12-basho windows.
The discussion focused on the following signed means, classifying Makuuchi
at the end endpoint:

| Window | Full history | Windows starting January 2016 onward |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 basho | +1.15 | +0.25 |
| 6 basho | +6.83 | +1.46 |
| 12 basho | +13.76 | +3.57 |

A shorter window may give a smaller average contribution, as illustrated by
this experiment. That is not a universal rule: shortening a window can also
remove adjustments that would have cancelled. The author retained 12 basho
as a useful sustained-period check rather than selecting the most favourable
window. Twelve represented basho is approximately two years; cancellations
can make its calendar duration longer.

The +3.57 recent figure is an average, not a bound on each observation. The
percentage distributions were subsequently inspected to address that distinction.

## The headline result: recent Makuuchi and Juryo

For every continuous 12-basho window starting at or after January 2016, calculate:

```text
percentage = 100 × net signed normalisation contribution / end rating
```

Division is determined at the end endpoint. Wrestlers need not have remained
in that division throughout the window. The result is:

| Division | Wrestler-window observations | Lowest | Highest | Within ±1% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Makuuchi | 2,099 | -0.34% | +0.77% | 2,099 |
| Juryo | 1,323 | -0.38% | +0.84% | 1,323 |

Every observation is within ±1%, using unrounded calculations and inclusive
boundaries. The largest recent Juryo ratio, for example, is Chiyootori's
+17.132-point contribution against an end rating of 2032.300, about +0.843%.

There are **51 distinct sliding windows**, advancing one basho at a time, from
January 2016–January 2018 through July 2024–July 2026. Both divisions cover all
51. This is complete coverage of eligible observations in the chosen period,
not 3,422 independent statistical tests: adjacent windows overlap in 11 basho,
and continuously represented wrestlers sharing endpoints receive the same
common adjustment. Their rating denominators differ.

The January 2016 cutoff is an exploratory choice and the principal softness
in using this as a headline. It is not a predeclared or statistically selected
boundary. The result establishes what happened in that period, not a probability
of future exceptions. The full-history analysis must remain alongside it.

## Full-history context, including all divisions

Using the same continuous 12-basho definition over the entire represented
record gives:

| Division at end | Observations | Mean signed percentage | Within ±1% | Within ±5% | Highest |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Makuuchi | 8,567 | +0.58% | 76.6% | 100.0% | +3.13% |
| Juryo | 5,421 | +0.63% | 74.5% | 100.0% | +3.38% |
| Makushita | 23,233 | +0.75% | 70.4% | 100.0% | +4.15% |
| Sandanme | 37,467 | +0.91% | 63.7% | 100.0% | +4.85% |
| Jonidan | 35,577 | +1.10% | 54.5% | 99.5% | +6.33% |
| Jonokuchi | 3,235 | +1.00% | 57.0% | 98.7% | +6.40% |
| All | 113,500 | +0.90% | 63.5% | 99.8% | +6.40% |

Only 219 observations fall outside ±5%: 176 in Jonidan and 43 in Jonokuchi.
All are on the positive side. The overall negative minimum is about -0.91%.
The largest positive observation is Shimenishiki's July 1991–July 1993 window:
+68.986 points against 1077.940, or +6.400%.

The author was comfortable treating contributions below 1% as negligible on
the familiar published scale, but wanted closer consideration around 5%.
These benchmarks emerged during interpretation; they were not predeclared
statistical tests. They made the recent upper-division result reassuring,
while the all-division result made the exceptional cases explicit.

The author accepts the lowest-division exceptions in the context of already
recognised difficulties with Elo-based interpretation below roughly Jd100.
Related earlier discussion appears in [lower-rank problems](../../equelo/docs/equelo%20docs/lower_rank_problems.md)
and the [initial-rating audit](../../equelo/docs/2026-06-27%20Initial%20Rating%20Audit.md).
The normalisation experiment itself neither tests predictive accuracy there
nor establishes that every percentage exception lies below that precise rank.
The separate question of why to publish Elo ratings in that difficult region
remains open; this investigation does not resolve it.

## What resolves the TBD, and what remains open

The investigation is sufficient to replace the TBD with an explicit judgement
of acceptability. It does not prove that the original expectation holds without
exception. Normalisation can appreciably affect individual reported changes;
the account should continue to say that those changes include population
adjustments as well as bout updates.

At the same time, the relational interpretation, signed cancellation, recent
upper-division evidence and full-history context explain why we are not overly
concerned about its effect on the usefulness of the ratings. No further
experiment is required to justify accepting the observed effects. Alternative
origins, cutoffs, windows, populations, lifetime accounting or counterfactual
replays could support other questions; they are not unfinished requirements
of this investigation.

Future readers should be able to distinguish:

- mathematical invariance under an instantaneous common shift;
- measured accounting contributions over selected windows;
- the author's benchmarks and judgement of practical importance; and
- untested claims about alternative rating histories or future outcomes.

The intended summary for the overall appraisal is:

> Population normalisation can contribute appreciably to particular reported
> rating changes, although positive and negative adjustments substantially
> cancel over time. Across all 51 recent sliding 12-basho windows, every eligible
> Makuuchi and Juryo observation had a net contribution within ±1% of its end
> rating. The full-history analysis records the larger exceptions. Having
> examined these results, we regard the observed effects as acceptable and find
> no sufficient reason to change Elo-89. Reported changes nevertheless include
> population normalisation and should not be described as wholly bout-derived.

## Reproduction and supporting records

Run from the repository root. The main diagnostic reads live-store History by
default, or accepts the explicit ZIP alternative shown here:

```powershell
python -m src.analysis.elo89_normalisation --history-zip "files/output/Historys/1989_01 to 2026_11.zip"
python -m src.analysis.elo89_normalisation.signed_results
python -m src.analysis.elo89_normalisation.division_percentages
```

Outputs default to `files/output/analysis/elo89_normalisation`. The main command
reads the existing production Elo-89 artifacts; supplementary commands read
the resulting diagnostic CSVs. They do not rerun the rating model. Their
manifests record sources and hashes so a changed production run can be
distinguished from the run described here.

- [Proposal](Proposal.md): original diagnostic definitions and scope.
- [Implementation record](Implementation.md): accounting and verification.
- [Signed findings](Signed%20Findings.md): detailed signed totals and percentiles.
- [Full-history division report](../../../../files/output/analysis/elo89_normalisation/division_percentages.md).
- [Recent Makuuchi/Juryo report](../../../../files/output/analysis/elo89_normalisation/recent_sekitori_percentages.md), with exact sample rules and counts.

The May-intake check was an exploratory calculation from the saved active
snapshots, separating never-before-seen IDs from returning IDs and excluding
the initial January 1989 population. It was not added as a dedicated output of
the diagnostic package. The window-count check deduplicated start/end dates in
the reproducible `recent_sekitori_windows.csv` output. Neither supporting
observation is an independent predictive experiment.
