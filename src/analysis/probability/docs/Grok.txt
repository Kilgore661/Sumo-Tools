The report is a very clean, well-structured, and intellectually honest piece of exploratory analysis. It does exactly what it sets out to do: run a **minimalist** rating-based system on real sumo bout data and rigorously check whether the resulting probabilities are empirically well-calibrated.

Here’s my overall assessment:

### Strengths (this is genuinely good work)

1. **Crystal-clear scoping and intellectual honesty**
   - The requirements document is excellent. It explicitly says “we’re not trying to build the best possible sumo model” and “we’re only testing whether a stupidly simple rating system can produce usable probabilities.”
   - It repeatedly acknowledges the non-goals and constraints. That level of self-discipline is rare and valuable.

2. **Focus on calibration as the primary success criterion**
   - This is the correct framing. In probabilistic forecasting, **calibration** matters far more than discrimination when the goal is “meaningful win probabilities.”
   - Using both raw Brier + binned reliability/resolution/uncertainty decomposition is exactly the right toolkit.

3. **Very responsible interpretation of results**
   - They don’t overclaim. They explicitly carve out the central region (0.35–0.75) where there is adequate data support and say “this is where we can actually evaluate it.”
   - They quantify the statistical resolution of the data (~±2%) and show that calibration error (~0.5%) is much smaller than that. That’s a mature way to think about it.
   - The tail-sparsity discussion is spot-on. Most rating systems look worse in the tails precisely because there’s almost no data there.

4. **Modest but real signal**
   - A Brier skill score of +2.34% doesn’t sound impressive, but in a domain where the base rate is ~50% and most bouts are fairly even (std dev of predicted p is only 0.081), that’s actually respectable for such a simple model.
   - The reliability term being tiny (0.000058) is impressive — the probabilities really are well-calibrated where it matters.

5. **Clean experimental design**
   - Sequential (online) updating, pre-bout predictions only, constant initial rating, ignoring fusen/blank bouts — all defensible and transparent choices.
   - Using the actual historical order prevents look-ahead bias.

### Minor weaknesses / things worth noting

- The improvement over baseline is small (as expected given how concentrated the probabilities are around 0.5). In sumo, most matches really are close to even, especially within the same division on a given day. A simple rating system can’t extract much more signal than that without additional features (which the experiment deliberately forbids).

- The choice of q=850 and the specific K-factor schedule (divisional, from an Elo FIDE json) feels a bit arbitrary, but since this is exploratory and they’re not claiming optimality, it’s fine. The report treats the parameters as fixed for this run.

- No sensitivity analysis is shown in the provided documents (e.g. varying q or initial rating b). That might exist elsewhere in the codebase, but from the report alone it looks like a single run. For a more robust “is this approach viable?” answer, a small parameter sweep would strengthen it.

- The report doesn’t discuss rating drift or era effects at all (correctly, per the non-goals), but in practice sumo has had major rule/weight/training changes over 68 years, so some of the signal might be polluted by non-stationarity. Again, that’s outside scope.

### What I make of the core finding

The experiment successfully shows that **yes**, even an extremely simple scalar-rating system (Elo-like, updated sequentially, constant initialization) produces probabilities that are **well-calibrated** over the vast majority of bouts (~96% of the data).

In other words: sumo outcomes are noisy enough, and the rating signal is clean enough, that a 1950s-era chess rating method applied blindly still gives you probabilities you can roughly trust in the practically relevant range.

That’s a nice negative result in a good way — it shows the baseline is surprisingly strong. It sets a very clear floor for what more complex models (incorporating age, weight difference, recent form, hikiotoshi specialist vs yorikiri specialist, etc.) would need to meaningfully beat.

You're right — I should have addressed **rating drift** (or inflation/deflation) more directly, especially since the requirements explicitly call it out as a non-goal and the experiment runs over a very long window (1958–2026, nearly 70 years of sumo history).

### Why drift is a legitimate concern in principle
Sumo has changed substantially over decades: average rikishi size and strength have increased, training methods evolved, the sport professionalized further, and there have been shifts in competition structure, promotion rules, and even cultural factors affecting participation. If the underlying "true strength" distribution of the entire pool shifts over time (non-stationarity), a pure Elo-style system can exhibit **global drift** — the average rating level slowly rises or falls as points flow through the closed system.

In many rating systems (especially chess), people worry that this makes absolute ratings incomparable across eras. A 1500 rating in 1960 might not mean the same thing as a 1500 in 2020.

### Why it arguably doesn't hurt (much) for this experiment's purpose
The core output here is **not absolute ratings or cross-era comparisons** — it's the **win probability derived from the current rating difference** at the moment of each bout.

The probability formula depends **only on the difference** (Δ = r1 − r2) and the scale parameter q:

p = 1 / (1 + 10^(-Δ / q))

If the entire rating pool drifts upward or downward together (a roughly uniform additive shift), the **differences** between active rikishi at any given time remain largely unaffected. When two wrestlers meet in a given basho, their ratings have both been updated through the same recent history against the same evolving pool. The relative gap that matters for that specific prediction stays intact.

In other words:
- Global inflation/deflation primarily affects the **level** of ratings.
- It tends to cancel out (or at least not systematically bias) when computing **differences** for contemporaneous matches.

This is exactly why the report can defensibly treat drift as out-of-scope: the experiment is testing whether rating **differences** produce well-calibrated probabilities in the bouts where they are actually used. It is not trying to say "Yokozuna A in 1975 was stronger than Yokozuna B in 2015."

### Caveats where drift could still matter
Even if global drift largely cancels for differences, there are subtler ways non-stationarity could leak in:
- **Differential drift** — If certain subgroups (e.g., lower divisions vs. makuuchi, or newer entrants vs. veterans) drift at different rates, then cross-group matches could have slightly distorted probabilities.
- **Changing variance** in the strength distribution over time — If the spread of abilities narrows or widens (e.g., due to better scouting or heavier athletes concentrating talent), the same rating difference might map to a different true win probability.
- **Era-specific shocks** — Major rule changes, weight class effects, or periods of dominance by a few rikishi could introduce temporary biases that the constant-K or divisional-K update doesn't fully absorb.

However, the excellent calibration results (very low reliability error in the well-supported central region) suggest that any such effects were not large enough to badly miscalibrate the probabilities where most bouts actually occur. If drift were systematically distorting the mapping from Δ to p, we would expect to see clearer patterns of over- or under-confidence in the binned calibration plots.

### Bottom line
The report's decision to ignore drift is reasonable and consistent with its minimalist scope. Because probabilities are generated from **relative differences within the contemporaneous pool**, uniform or slowly varying global drift tends to be self-canceling for the purpose of empirical calibration testing.

This is one of the quiet strengths of online Elo-style systems: they are surprisingly robust to slow background shifts precisely because they are purely relative and adaptive. The fact that the model still achieves tight calibration over 600k+ bouts across nearly seven decades is evidence that the approach works better than one might naively expect given all the real-world changes in sumo.

If the team ever wants to probe this further without violating the "simple scalar ratings only" constraint, a natural extension would be to look at calibration **split by era** (e.g., pre- vs. post-1990) or by division. But as written, the current stance is defensible.
