
# Expert summary of (M_{\mathrm{Elo}})

We use a standard Elo rating system with the following characteristics:

* Ratings take values in (R = \mathbb{R}) and are initialized to a constant (b \in B).
* Expected scores are computed using the canonical logistic formula with fixed parameter (q \in Q).
* Ratings are updated sequentially over a chronologically ordered history of bouts.
* Updates are local: only the two participants in a bout have their ratings changed.
* Ratings persist unchanged in periods of inactivity; once a rikishi ceases to appear, their rating remains fixed.

The only domain-specific modification is:

* The K-factor is not constant, but is a function of chii:
  [
  k = K(c)
  ]
  where (c \in C) is the chii of the rikishi at the time of the bout.

No other modifications to the Elo model are made:

* no volatility or uncertainty terms,
* no rating decay,
* no population-level normalization during the time evolution,
* no special handling of entry or exit beyond initialization at (b).

# Formal definition of (M_{\mathrm{Elo}})

## 1. Basic sets and constants

Let:

* (\mathcal{I}) be the set of rikishi

* (\mathcal{T}) be a totally ordered set of bout-times

* (R = \mathbb{R}) be the rating space

* (O = {0,1}) be the outcome space

* (B \subseteq R), with fixed (b \in B) (initial rating)

* (Q \subseteq \mathbb{R}_{>0}), with fixed (q \in Q) (scale parameter)

* (C) be the set of chii

* (K \subseteq \mathbb{R}_{>0}), the k-factor space

* (k : C \to K) be the k-factor function

---

## 2. History

A history is given by:

### (a) Bout sequence

[
b : \mathcal{T} \to \mathcal{I} \times \mathcal{I} \times O
]

For each (t \in \mathcal{T}):

[
b(t) = (i_t, j_t, o_t)
]

where (o_t) is the outcome for (i_t).

---

### (b) Chii function

[
c : \mathcal{I} \times \mathcal{T} \to C
]

giving the chii of each rikishi at time (t) (when defined).

---

## 3. Rating state

A rating state at time (t) is a function:

[
r_t : \mathcal{I} \to R
]

Initial condition:

[
r_0(i) = b \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{I}
]

---

## 4. Expected score

For (e, e' \in R), define:

[
E(e,e') = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(e' - e)/q}}
]

---

## 5. Single-bout update

Let (t \in \mathcal{T}) and (b(t) = (i,j,o)).

Let:

[
e_i = r_{t^-}(i), \quad e_j = r_{t^-}(j), \quad c_i = c(i,t)
]

Define:

[
\Delta_t = k(c_i)\bigl(o - E(e_i,e_j)\bigr)
]

Then:

[
r_t(i) = e_i + \Delta_t
]
[
r_t(j) = e_j - \Delta_t
]

and for all (m \notin {i,j}):

[
r_t(m) = r_{t^-}(m)
]

---

## 6. Locality

For each bout:

* only the two participants are updated,
* all other rikishi retain their previous ratings.

---

## 7. Persistence

Ratings persist through time unless changed by participation in a bout.

If a rikishi does not appear in any future bouts, their rating remains constant for all subsequent times.

---

## 8. Basho boundaries

Let (\mathcal{B}) be the ordered set of basho.

Define:

[
r^{\mathrm{end}}_b(i), \quad r^{\mathrm{start}}_b(i)
]

with:

[
r^{\mathrm{start}}_{b+1}(i) = r^{\mathrm{end}}_b(i)
]

for all continuing rikishi.

Between basho, ratings do not change.

---

## 9. Elo calculation

Given:

* bout sequence (b),
* chii function (c),
* constants (b \in B), (q \in Q),
* k-factor function (k : C \to K),

the Elo calculation is the sequence:

[
(r_t)_{t \in \mathcal{T}}
]

obtained by iterating the update rule over all (t \in \mathcal{T}).

---

## 10. Summary

(M_{\mathrm{Elo}}) is the model in which ratings evolve by sequential application of the canonical Elo update rule with:

* fixed (b) and (q),
* k-factor (k(c)) determined by chii,
* persistent ratings,
* and strictly local updates.

Yes. I agree with those ideas.

They are the right next step because they test the model, not the prototype implementation details.

What you are proposing is really a first validation programme for (M_{\mathrm{Elo}}). It separates into four kinds of question.

## 1. Invariant checks

These are the most basic and should be done first.

* Does the mean active rating remain equal to (b) throughout a pass, up to floating-point noise?
* Are bout updates exactly zero-sum apart from floating-point error?
* When a joiner is added at (b), does the mean remain unchanged?
* When a retiree is removed and compensated for, does the mean remain unchanged?

These are almost contractual tests of the model.

## 2. Scale checks

These address your “tiny adjustment” claim.

* Distribution of retiree adjustments (\delta/n)
* Maximum absolute adjustment ever applied
* Typical adjustment by year or decade
* Cumulative effect on a continuing rikishi over a career
* Comparison of retirement adjustments with ordinary bout-induced Elo changes

That last one is especially useful. “Tiny” should probably mean tiny relative to a normal one-bout update.

## 3. Face-validity checks

These address whether the output looks like something an informed person would expect.

* Do ratings broadly track chii?
* Is the top end mostly occupied by yokozuna / ōzeki-level rikishi?
* Are lower divisions generally lower-rated?
* Are obvious anomalies rare, local, and explainable?

I would treat these as heuristics, not hard requirements. “Yokozuna highest” is not a theorem of the model; it is an empirical smell test.

## 4. Transient / stabilisation checks

This is a very good question.

Since everyone starts at (b), early values are structurally artificial. So you want to know when the system becomes informative.

Possible ways to operationalise “stabilised”:

* mean rating by chii stops drifting much from basho to basho
* ordering of chii-level averages becomes mostly stable
* distributional summaries by chii become approximately stationary
* ratings of long-active rikishi become insensitive to moving the epoch earlier
* rerunning from different early cutoffs yields similar outputs after some later date

That last one is especially strong. If results from, say, 1958 and 1965 initialisations are nearly the same by some later period, that is good evidence that the transient has washed out.

## Additional ideas

A few more seem worthwhile.

### Sensitivity to policy choices

Since you are explicitly discussing “Matters Arising”, test whether those choices matter.

* retirees persist forever vs are removed with compensation
* equal redistribution vs weighted redistribution
* lazy entry-at-first-bout vs explicit basho-start initialisation

If outputs barely move, that is valuable evidence that these are commentary-level issues.

### Sensitivity to parameters

Not to optimize yet, just to understand behaviour.

* different (b)
* different (q)
* different k-factor schedules

You are not trying to choose them yet. You are asking whether the model behaves qualitatively robustly.

### Observation-point choice

Because you care about basho boundaries:

* compare end-of-basho ratings
* compare start-of-basho ratings
* compare daily trajectories only as internal machinery

This helps keep the eventual project aligned with the quantities you actually care about.

### Chii-level aggregation

Since the fixed-point layer will be about chii-level quantities anyway, inspect early:

* mean rating by chii over time
* spread within each chii over time
* count of observations per chii
* whether some chii are too sparse for stable inference

### Historical plausibility

Not “does it reproduce history perfectly?”, but:

* major dominant rikishi should look dominant
* long weak careers should not drift implausibly high
* promotions/demotions should usually coincide with rating movement in the expected direction

## One caution

I would distinguish carefully between:

* **properties of the model**
* **properties of one chosen dataset and parameterisation**

For example:

* “mean remains (b)” is a model property
* “yokozuna are highest” is an empirical check on a run

That separation will help later when you write requirements.

## Recommended order

I would do the experiment in this order:

1. invariants
2. size of retiree adjustments
3. transient/stabilisation
4. face-validity of outputs
5. sensitivity to policy choices

That order gives you confidence before interpretation.

## A crisp way to state the experiment

You could say:

> First implement (M_{\mathrm{Elo}}) and subject it to invariant, scale, transient, and face-validity checks. In particular, test whether the mean remains equal to (b), whether retiree adjustments are negligible in practice, whether the induced ratings correlate sensibly with chii, and whether there is an initial transient after which the system behaves stably.

That seems very well aligned with where you are.
