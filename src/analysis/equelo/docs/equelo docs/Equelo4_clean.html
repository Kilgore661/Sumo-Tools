What Equelo Is For

Equelo is strongest as a descriptive tool for exploring performance over time.

For an individual rikishi, an Eq trajectory can show rise, decline, recovery, stagnation, or sudden change. It gives a continuous account where the banzuke gives a stepped and sometimes delayed account. This is especially useful for cases involving injury, return from absence, rapid promotion, or a rikishi finding his level.

Equelo is also useful for comparing rikishi within the same broad period. Since those rikishi share much of the same rating environment, the comparison is relatively local.

Cross-era comparison is more delicate. Equelo is designed to make such comparisons more meaningful than plain Elo would, because the rating scale is normalised and the initial ratings are rank-calibrated. But cross-era comparisons remain model-based. Changes in the sport, the population, recruitment, training, scheduling, and the structure of the banzuke can all affect interpretation.

The most delicate use is using Eq to contradict a chii-based fact. If Eq says that a lower-ranked rikishi is stronger than a higher-ranked rikishi, that may be interesting. It may even be persuasive. But the strength of the claim depends on the context: the data support, the period, the rikishi involved, and whether the result is robust under reasonable changes to the method.

The Honest Claim

The honest claim for Equelo is modest but worthwhile.

Equelo does not prove who was truly best. It does not recover missing bouts. It does not eliminate judgement from the interpretation of sumo. It does not turn the banzuke into a single perfect numerical scale.

What it does is combine recorded results and banzuke evidence in a transparent rating system designed for an open historical population. It controls rating-scale drift, replaces arbitrary common initial ratings with calibrated chii-based initial ratings, and makes explicit where the data are strong enough to support direct estimates and where completion rules are needed.

That makes Eq useful. It provides a coherent way to ask questions such as:

  • How did a rikishi’s performance rating evolve over time?
  • How does the rating story compare with the banzuke story?
  • Where does the model agree with traditional rank-based intuition?
  • Where does it disagree?
  • When it disagrees, are we seeing something real, or seeing a limitation of the model?

Those last questions are not failures of the system. They are part of the point. Equelo is interesting not only when it confirms what we already think, but also when it produces a result that forces us to ask whether the model has found a genuine feature of the data or exposed one of its own weaknesses.
