What is Equelo?

Equelo, or Eq, is an Elo-like rating system for Grand Sumo. It starts from the same basic idea as Elo: a rikishi has a numerical rating, and that rating changes after a bout according to the result and the rating of the opponent. Beating a stronger opponent is worth more than beating a weaker one; losing to a weaker opponent costs more than losing to a stronger one. Eq keeps that idea, but it changes the surrounding machinery so that the ratings are better suited to historical sumo data.

The short version is this: Equelo uses Elo-style bout updates, banzuke ranks as calibrated starting information, and a mean-conservation rule to prevent the rating scale from drifting. It is intended to be transparent and useful, but it is not a claim to have discovered the hidden true ability of every rikishi. It is a defined model, and its outputs have to be understood as the outputs of that model.

This distinction matters. Equelo is not pure Elo, and it is not the banzuke in numerical form. It is an attempt to combine two kinds of evidence:

  • recorded bout results, which say what happened on the dohyo; and
  • banzuke ranks, which contain information about previous performance, even when the underlying bout records are incomplete.

Where the bout records are complete, Equelo behaves like an Elo-style performance rating system with additional controls. Where the bout records are incomplete, Equelo uses the banzuke as calibrated evidence rather than pretending that missing information does not matter.

Why Plain Elo Is Not Enough

Plain Elo is attractive because it is simple and transparent. Once the parameters and starting ratings have been chosen, ratings change because of results. A rikishi gains or loses points according to whether he wins and how strong his opponent was expected to be. This gives a structured account of performance against opposition rather than a simple count of wins.

However, historical sumo creates problems for a direct Elo calculation.

The first problem is the starting point. In a simple Elo system, every competitor is often assigned the same initial rating, say $b=1000$. That is deliberately ignorant: it says that at the start of the calculation, we do not know who is strong and who is weak. This may be a reasonable convention in some settings, but it is not a good description of a banzuke. A yokozuna, an ozeki, a mid-maegashira, and a new juryo rikishi are not equally strong merely because the rating calculation has just begun. The system will move them apart over time, but that means early ratings are partly contaminated by the arbitrary common start.

The second problem is missing history. We have banzuke records going back through the historical period of interest, so we know who was ranked where. But bout records are not equally complete across divisions. Sekitori results are available much further back than complete lower-division results. This matters because a rikishi who first appears in the sekitori results did not come from nowhere. He reached juryo by fighting in the lower divisions. If those lower-division bouts are missing, plain Elo has no direct way to know what his rating should be when he enters the recorded sekitori pool.

The third problem is population change. Individual Elo bouts are normally zero-sum: one rikishi gains what the other loses. But the active population is not fixed. New rikishi enter, old rikishi retire, and the number of active rikishi changes. If entrants bring points into the system and retirees remove some other amount, the total number of rating points in the active population can drift. If the scale drifts, comparisons across time become less reliable.

These problems are not small details. They affect the interpretation of the ratings. If a rating system is meant to compare rikishi across many decades, it needs to say how it handles incomplete records, arbitrary starting assumptions, and an open population.

The Core Idea

Equelo addresses these problems by changing the way rikishi enter and leave the rating system.

Instead of assuming that every rikishi starts at the same arbitrary rating, Equelo assigns entrant ratings according to chii: the banzuke rank held by the rikishi at the relevant point. The question then becomes: what rating should a given chii have?

The answer is not chosen by hand. Equelo estimates chii-based initial ratings by a fixed-point process. In broad terms:

  1. start with provisional initial ratings for each chii;
  2. run the Elo-like rating calculation through the historical results;
  3. aggregate the resulting ratings by chii;
  4. use those aggregated chii values as the next provisional initial ratings;
  5. repeat until the chii values stop changing, or change only negligibly.

If this process converges, the result is a self-consistent mapping from chii to rating. A yokozuna initial rating is not asserted in advance; it is the rating that the system settles on for yokozuna in the context of the available historical data and the model assumptions. The same is true for ozeki, maegashira, juryo, makushita, and so on.

This solves the start-up problem in a principled way. It does not say that the banzuke is perfect. It says that the banzuke is evidence. A rank is the result of previous performance and judgement, and therefore contains information that should not be thrown away merely because some bout records are missing.

Mean Conservation

The fixed-point idea deals with initial ratings. It does not, by itself, solve rating inflation.

Suppose there are $n$ active rikishi and the baseline rating is $b$. If every rikishi initially has rating $b$, then the total number of rating points in the active system is

\[ nb \]

and the average rating is $b$.

In a closed population, zero-sum bout updates preserve that total. But sumo is not a closed population. Rikishi enter and leave. If a new low-ranked rikishi enters with rating points, some of those points may move upward through wins and promotions. When higher-ranked rikishi retire, the points removed from the active system need not balance the points introduced. The result can be an upward or downward drift in the rating scale.

Equelo therefore uses a mean-conservation rule. When the active population changes, ratings are adjusted so that the average active rating remains at the baseline value $b$. Equivalently, if there are $n$ active rikishi, the active rating pool is kept centred on total mass $nb$.

This adjustment is not a claim about any individual bout. It is a scale-preserving rule. It says that changes in the number and composition of active rikishi should not, by themselves, move the meaning of the rating scale. Without such a rule, the rating system could confuse population mechanics with performance.

This is one of the places where the model is doing visible work. Mean conservation is a deliberate modelling choice. It is not something observed directly in the world. The justification is that, without it, the rating scale can drift for reasons that are not the performance of any particular rikishi.

Rank-Based Initial Ratings

The fixed-point procedure gives Equelo a way to infer initial ratings from chii. Conceptually, the procedure can be written as an iteration. Let $I_t(c)$ be the initial rating assigned to chii $c$ at iteration $t$. Running the rating system with the initial map $I_t$ produces ratings, which can then be aggregated by chii to produce a new map:

\[ I_{t+1}(c) = A_c(S(I_t)) \]

Here $S(I_t)$ denotes the simulation run using the current initial-rating map, and $A_c$ denotes the aggregation rule used to extract a representative value for chii $c$ from the simulated ratings.

A fixed point is reached when, for practical purposes,

\[ I_{t+1}(c) \approx I_t(c) \]

for all chii being estimated. At that point the chii ratings are self-consistent: using them as inputs reproduces them as outputs.

That does not make them metaphysically true. It means they are stable under the defined process. This is still useful. If the method says that a given rank has a certain rating value, that value is not arbitrary. It is the value implied by the interaction of the banzuke, the bout records, the update rule, and the normalisation rule.

Historical Coverage and the 1989 Problem

The historical data are not uniform. There are banzuke records throughout the period of interest, but complete lower-division bout results are only available from a later point. This creates a choice.

One possibility is to start the entire rating system only when full lower-division data are available. That gives a cleaner dataset, but throws away decades of sekitori history.

Another possibility is to start earlier and use only the available results. That preserves the sekitori history, but leaves lower-division ratings poorly anchored before the lower-division records become complete.

Equelo takes a third approach. It uses the complete-data period to learn what ranks mean across the full banzuke, including the lower divisions. It then uses those rank values when earlier incomplete records require them. For example, if a makushita rikishi appears in an early sekitori-adjacent bout, the system can assign him a rating based on the calibrated value of his rank, even though his full lower-division history is not available.

This is a reconstruction method, not time travel. The later record is not used to invent missing earlier bouts. It is used to calibrate the relationship between rank and rating. The earlier partial record is then interpreted using that calibration.

This should be stated plainly because it is an obvious objection. Equelo sometimes uses information learned from the better-recorded later period to interpret the worse-recorded earlier period. That is true. The justification is that the banzuke exists throughout, and the later complete record gives us a way to estimate what those ranks mean on the rating scale.

The resulting early lower-division ratings should therefore be treated as model-derived estimates. They are not directly observed historical facts. Their reliability depends on the assumption that later rank-rating relationships are useful guides to earlier rank-rating relationships.

Support and Completion

The fixed-point idea suggests that every chii can receive its own independently estimated rating. In practice, this is not always safe.

Some chii have plenty of observations and are well supported. Others are rare, historically contingent, or populated by only a handful of rikishi. In those cases the fixed-point process can produce values that are technically implied by the iteration but not substantively credible. A rank with very few observations may be dominated by one unusual rikishi, one unusual basho, or the mechanics of the iteration itself.

This is not hypothetical. Earlier experiments produced pathological values for sparsely supported lower-division ranks. A rank with almost no support could acquire an absurd initial rating because the system had too little independent evidence to discipline the estimate.

The current production approach therefore distinguishes between supported and unsupported chii. Supported chii receive direct estimates. Unsupported chii are completed by a defined rule, typically by borrowing from the nearest supported chii in rank order. This means the final entrant-rating map is not a raw fixed-point table. It is a completed map: partly direct estimates, partly deterministic completions.

This is a pragmatic intervention, but it is not a hidden one. It should be part of the definition of the system. Equelo, as actually used, includes support filtering and completion. The goal is not to pretend that every rank has enough information to stand on its own. The goal is to use independent estimates where they are supported and avoid giving false precision where they are not.

What Equelo Produces

Equelo produces several different kinds of output, and they should not be confused.

The first is the entrant initial-rating map. This gives the rating assigned to a rikishi when the system needs an initial value based on his chii. It is a runtime artefact used by the rating process itself. In the current implementation, this map is keyed by chii ordinal and contains the final initial ratings used by the simulation.

The second is the master chii initial-rating map. This is the explanatory version of the entrant map. It records the chii, its ordinal, the initial rating, and information about whether the value is directly estimated or completed from another supported chii. This is the artefact that should be used when explaining how the initial-rating system works.

The third is the day-end rating output. This records the ratings of rikishi after the day’s bouts have been processed. These ratings are the main historical time series: they allow us to look at the rise, decline, recovery, or stagnation of individual rikishi.

There may also be public explanatory artefacts derived from these outputs. Care is needed here. A smoothed monotone table of “typical” ratings by rank may look attractive, but if the raw model says that some lower rank has a higher observed or estimated rating than a rank above it, smoothing that away is not a description. It is a normative curve: what the ranks feel as if they should be, not what the model found.

For that reason, any public rank-value artefact should tell the truth about the model output. If the rank-rating curve is not monotone, the artefact should show that, and the prose should say that it is surprising. The anomaly is not something to hide. It is something to analyse.

What the Ratings Mean

An Eq rating is a model-derived comparative rating. It is not an absolute measure of ability. Like Elo, its meaning lies in relationships between ratings, especially differences between ratings. A rikishi with a higher rating has stronger evidence of performance within the rating system than a rikishi with a lower rating.

That last phrase matters: within the rating system. Eq ratings are not independent facts about the world. They are outputs of a defined process. That process uses results, rank-based initialisation, support filtering, completion, and mean conservation. The ratings mean what they mean under those assumptions.

This makes the ratings useful, but it also limits them. If Eq says that one rikishi is ahead of another, that is evidence generated by the model. It is not a final judgement. If Eq disagrees with the banzuke, the disagreement is interesting. It may indicate that the banzuke is slow to reflect a change in performance. It may indicate that Eq has noticed something useful. It may also indicate that the model is being distorted by missing data, rank composition, population churn, or some other artefact.

The purpose of defining Equelo carefully is to make those disagreements interpretable. If we know exactly what the system does, we can ask better questions when it produces surprising results.

Analysing the Raw Results

The raw results are not perfectly tidy. In particular, the rank-rating curve is not always monotone. One would naturally expect higher ranks to have higher ratings than lower ranks. Broadly, that is true. But there are local exceptions.

The lower maegashira ranks appear to be one such region. There is a local minimum around the lower maegashira area: ranks below that point may receive higher Eq values than ranks immediately above it. This is surprising. It is not what a simple reading of the banzuke would suggest.

There are also lower-division problems involving sparse ranks. If a rank is represented by too few observations, the raw fixed-point process can produce values that are implausibly high or otherwise unstable. These cases are a warning that the fixed-point process is not magic. It needs sufficient support to produce estimates that deserve confidence.

These anomalies create an interpretive problem. They may reflect real structure in the data. They may reflect the composition of rikishi at particular ranks: promoted rikishi, demoted rikishi, injured returners, short-career wrestlers, boundary effects, or churn. They may reflect schedule effects or missing records. Or they may reflect defects in the model.

The correct response is not to smooth them away and pretend the model produced the shape we expected. The correct response is to report the raw behaviour, identify the places where it conflicts with expectation, and then investigate possible explanations.

Epistemology and Pragmatism

Equelo sits between two pressures.

On one side is the epistemological question: how much of this is a principled mathematical account of performance, and how much is a pragmatic construction designed to make an imperfect model usable? Very few mathematical accounts of the world are exact, and statistical or rating systems are especially dependent on modelling choices. Mean conservation, support filtering, and completion rules are not facts about sumo. They are decisions about how to represent sumo in a rating system.

On the other side is the pragmatic question: how much does that matter? The purpose of the site is not to prove the final truth about sumo. It is to produce interesting, transparent, and repeatable ways of looking at sumo history. If Equelo gives useful trajectories, comparisons, and puzzles, then it has value even if it is not perfect.

The danger is overclaiming. If Eq is used to assert something bold on the basis of thin evidence, a critic can reasonably say that the conclusion is only as good as the model. If the model has known rough edges, those rough edges matter.

The answer is not to abandon the model, and not to hide the rough edges. The answer is to calibrate the claims:

  • When Eq follows the banzuke and common sense, it gives a useful numerical version of an already plausible story.
  • When Eq diverges from the banzuke, the divergence is a prompt for investigation, not an automatic discovery.
  • When Eq produces a surprising rank-level pattern, the pattern should be shown and analysed, not replaced by a smoother curve.
  • When the data are sparse, claims should be weaker.

This gives a sensible way to use the ratings. Equelo is a lens. A lens can reveal structure, but it can also distort. The point is to know the shape of the lens.

What Equelo Is For

Equelo is strongest as a descriptive tool for exploring performance over time.

For an individual rikishi, an Eq trajectory can show rise, decline, recovery, stagnation, or sudden change. It gives a continuous account where the banzuke gives a stepped and sometimes delayed account. This is especially useful for cases involving injury, return from absence, rapid promotion, or a rikishi finding his level.

Equelo is also useful for comparing rikishi within the same broad period. Since those rikishi share much of the same rating environment, the comparison is relatively local.

Cross-era comparison is more delicate. Equelo is designed to make such comparisons more meaningful than plain Elo would, because the rating scale is normalised and the initial ratings are rank-calibrated. But cross-era comparisons remain model-based. Changes in the sport, the population, recruitment, training, scheduling, and the structure of the banzuke can all affect interpretation.

The most delicate use is using Eq to contradict a chii-based fact. If Eq says that a lower-ranked rikishi is stronger than a higher-ranked rikishi, that may be interesting. It may even be persuasive. But the strength of the claim depends on the context: the data support, the period, the rikishi involved, and whether the result is robust under reasonable changes to the method.

The Honest Claim

The honest claim for Equelo is modest but worthwhile.

Equelo does not prove who was truly best. It does not recover missing bouts. It does not eliminate judgement from the interpretation of sumo. It does not turn the banzuke into a single perfect numerical scale.

What it does is combine recorded results and banzuke evidence in a transparent rating system designed for an open historical population. It controls rating-scale drift, replaces arbitrary common initial ratings with calibrated chii-based initial ratings, and makes explicit where the data are strong enough to support direct estimates and where completion rules are needed.

That makes Eq useful. It provides a coherent way to ask questions such as:

  • How did a rikishi’s performance rating evolve over time?
  • How does the rating story compare with the banzuke story?
  • Where does the model agree with traditional rank-based intuition?
  • Where does it disagree?
  • When it disagrees, are we seeing something real, or seeing a limitation of the model?

Those last questions are not failures of the system. They are part of the point. Equelo is interesting not only when it confirms what we already think, but also when it produces a result that forces us to ask whether the model has found a genuine feature of the data or exposed one of its own weaknesses.
