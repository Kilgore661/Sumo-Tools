Consider an Elo system for \(n\) players in a game with no draws. Suppose that we know each player’s true rating \(b_i\) and use it as their initial Elo rating. We then simulate a continuing series of games, choosing the pair of players for each game uniformly at random and generating the result according to their true ratings. The Elo ratings will not remain fixed at the true ratings. Starting them at the correct values means only that, initially, their expected changes are balanced; the results of actual games will still move them, and a fixed \(K\)-factor ensures that these movements never die away.

The ratings will tend to be pulled back when they move badly out of line with the players’ true strengths, so large departures are increasingly rare the larger they are. Nevertheless, no finite band around a player’s true rating is guaranteed to contain that player’s Elo rating forever. What eventually settles down is the general pattern of fluctuation. For any chosen band, the proportion of observations lying outside it should eventually level off at some value. For a reasonably wide band this value may be very small, but with a fixed \(K\) it will generally not be zero.

For very small values of \(K\), Cortez and Tossounian (2026) show that the average size of the long-run discrepancies from the true ratings is no greater than a constant times \(\sqrt{K}\). Thus reducing \(K\) by a factor of four halves this upper bound, although the exact size of the discrepancies depends on the players and the details of the system. This result does not limit how far an individual rating may occasionally wander. The fluctuations can be reduced by lowering \(K\), but they do not disappear merely because the simulation is run for longer.

For the interested reader

Some readers may be interested in the roles played by Elo’s two constant parameters \(K\) and \(q\). In the usual Elo convention, where a win has score 1 and a loss has score 0, the expected score for a rating difference \(x\) is \[ p(x)=\frac{1}{1+10^{-x/q}}. \] The number \(L\) measures the greatest slope of this curve. That greatest slope occurs at \(x=0\), giving \[ L=\max_x p'(x)=\frac{\ln 10}{4q}. \]

One of the hypotheses that must be satisfied in order to apply Cortez and Tossounian’s results as stated, translated into this usual 0-to-1 scoring convention, is \(KL<1\). Consequently, \[ K<\frac{1}{L}=\frac{4q}{\ln 10}, \qquad \sqrt{K}<2\sqrt{\frac{q}{\ln 10}}. \] For the common choice \(q=400\), this becomes \[ K<\frac{1600}{\ln 10}\approx 694.87, \qquad \sqrt{K}<\sqrt{\frac{1600}{\ln 10}}\approx 26.36. \] The statement about \(\sqrt K\) here is simply the result of taking the square root of the general condition on \(K\).

Their claim that the average long-run discrepancy from the true ratings is no greater than a constant times \(\sqrt K\) requires a further, much stricter condition. For example, suppose that \(q=400\), that the true ratings range from 1000 to 2800, and that their mean is 1900. After centring, the ratings range from \(-900\) to \(900\). Translating their explicit condition into the usual 0-to-1 scoring convention then gives \[ K\leq 3.62\times 10^{-7}. \] This is infeasibly small for a practical Elo simulation. It is a sufficient condition supplied by the proof, rather than a claim that the observed \(\sqrt K\) pattern must fail for every larger value of \(K\). If the ratings’ mean is not 1900, the numerical condition may be smaller still.
