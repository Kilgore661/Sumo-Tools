Grand Sumo (Ōzumō): A Structural Overview

Professional sumo is a centrally administered competition in which rikishi are arranged in a single ranking hierarchy, compete in regularly scheduled tournaments, and move upward or downward according to their results. Its basic cycle is: 

ranking list → tournament bouts → records → revised ranking list

All professional sumo is governed by the Japan Sumo Association. Rikishi belong to training organisations called stables, or heya, but compete as individuals rather than as stable teams. Stable membership affects training, recruitment and bout scheduling.

Grand Sumo, henceforth “sumo”, has many rules, but relatively few are absolute. Unless stated otherwise, the account below describes normal practice rather than inviolable rules.

The tournament cycle

Sumo holds six official tournaments, or honbasho, each year. They begin in every odd-numbered month, and each lasts 15 consecutive days.

Rikishi in the top two divisions normally compete once per day, giving them 15 bouts. Rikishi in the lower four divisions normally compete in seven bouts spread across the tournament.

A winning record is called kachi-koshi and a losing record make-koshi. Normally, eight wins produce kachi-koshi in the top two divisions and four wins produce it in the lower divisions. These records are the main inputs used to construct the ranking list for the next tournament.

A rikishi who is absent or has withdrawn is kyūjō. If he does not appear for a bout that has already been scheduled, the bout is not contested: this is fusen. The absent rikishi receives a loss by default, fusenpai, while his opponent receives a win by default, fusenshō. On subsequent days the absent rikishi is normally not scheduled, so those days do not produce further fusen results.

For ranking purposes, an ordinary win has the same numerical value regardless of the opponent or how the bout was won. Nevertheless, the strength of opposition can differ substantially between rikishi with the same record.

The ranking hierarchy

The official ranking list is called the banzuke. It places each active rikishi at a specific position in a single ordered hierarchy.

The hierarchy has six divisions, from highest to lowest:

  1. Makuuchi
  2. Jūryō (J)
  3. Makushita (Ms)
  4. Sandanme (Sd)
  5. Jonidan (Jd)
  6. Jonokuchi (Jk)

The boundary between Jūryō and Makushita is particularly important. Rikishi in Makuuchi and Jūryō are salaried professionals known collectively as sekitori. We sometimes use sub-sekitori for rikishi in the lower four divisions.

Promotion and demotion can occur within a division or across a division boundary. A strong winning record generally moves a rikishi upward, while a losing record generally moves him downward. The amount of movement is not mechanically fixed. It also depends on his previous position, the records of other rikishi and the places available in the relevant part of the banzuke.

The active population is finite at any particular basho but open through time: new rikishi enter, established rikishi retire, and the size and composition of the population change.

Chii

A position on the banzuke is called a chii. Most chii have three components:

  • a division or named rank;
  • a numerical level;
  • an East or West side.

For example, Ms30w means Makushita, level 30, West.

Within a category, level 1 is highest, followed by level 2, level 3 and so forth. At each level, East is immediately above West. Thus: 

Ms4w > Ms5e > Ms5w > Ms6e

Here > means “has the higher chii”.

The majority of Makuuchi rikishi are maegashira, abbreviated M. Their chii are ordered: 

M1e, M1w, M2e, M2w, …

The lowest maegashira number varies because the composition of Makuuchi is not constant.

Above maegashira are the named ranks, collectively known as san’yaku. In descending order they are: 

Yokozuna (Y)
Ōzeki (O)
Sekiwake (S)
Komusubi (K)

The same number-and-side notation is used. For example, Y1e is above Y1w, while additional yokozuna may occupy Y2e, Y2w and so forth.

The named ranks have special promotion and demotion rules. Consequently, movement through the top of the hierarchy cannot be described completely by a single mechanical rule based only on the latest record.

Bout scheduling

A tournament is not a complete round-robin competition. Rikishi do not face every other member of their division, and the Japan Sumo Association determines the pairing for each bout.

Rank is important in scheduling, particularly early in a tournament. As results accumulate, current performance also becomes important: successful rikishi are increasingly likely to face other successful rikishi. Division boundaries, stable membership and other constraints also affect which pairings are possible.

The schedule is therefore adaptive and non-random. Two rikishi can finish with identical records after facing substantially different sets of opponents.

This creates an important distinction:

  • the banzuke records the official ordering of rikishi;
  • tournament records count wins and losses;
  • a rating system can additionally account for the estimated strength of the opposition.

These are related views of performance, but they are not identical.
